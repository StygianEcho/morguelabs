Autopsy №003: Buying options for the big win

SPECIMEN
Long out-of-the-money options as a wealth strategy — lottery tickets, gamma-squeeze chasing, 0DTE lottos
CLAIM
Convexity: risk a little premium for unbounded upside. Catch one squeeze and retire.
VERDICT
DEAD
CAUSE OF DEATH
The variance risk premium. You are systematically overpaying for volatility, and the crashes you're waiting for don't come close to covering the bleed.
SAMPLE
VIX / VIX3M / S&P 500, 2006–2026 · plus the published retail-outcome literature
DATE OF DEATH
July 2026

The pitch

One screenshot: $6k into weekly calls, $1.2M out. The logic sounds like physics — options have capped downside and uncapped upside, so keep buying cheap lottery tickets and one asymmetric win pays for everything. The post-2021 refinement adds a mechanism: find the gamma squeeze, ride the dealers' forced hedging, be the flywheel instead of the fuel.

The test

Twenty years of S&P 500 volatility data, 2006–2026. We priced monthly out-of-the-money strangles off implied volatility and measured what systematically buying them earns — deliberately generously: our pricing ignored volatility skew, which in reality makes OTM options more expensive than we assumed. Everything below is the charitable version.

The result

The core fact of the options market, visible across two decades: implied volatility runs about 3.4 points above the volatility that subsequently happens, and that gap is positive 82% of all days. This is the variance risk premium — the standing fee the market charges people who want convexity. Everything else follows from it:

  • A systematic 5%-OTM monthly strangle loses roughly 68% per month on average.
  • A 10%-OTM strangle loses effectively everything, every month. Note the direction: further out-of-the-money — where the lottery tickets and the screenshots live — is worse, not better.
  • No crash rescues the sample. This isn't a good bet ruined by unlucky timing — we checked whether any single blow-up month (2008, 2020, any of them) carried the strategy. None did. The bleed is pervasive; the paydays are too small and too rare.
  • The escape hatches don't escape. The one regime where realized vol genuinely beats implied — term-structure backwardation, i.e., panic — is real but tiny (about 0.01 vol points), roughly one-tenth the size needed to cover the cost of owning options through it: the strangle still loses ~55% per month in the strategy's best regime.

What about the squeeze? As a repeatable strategy, gamma-squeeze chasing has a sample-size problem it cannot outrun: a handful of correlated famous events, counted after the fact, minus all the names that squeezed nobody and went to zero without a screenshot. The academic paper most often cited in its favor identifies squeeze events using data from the following 22 trading days — a look-ahead you cannot trade — books most of its excess return before the signal exists, draws from a universe of half-billion-dollar low-float names where real execution costs are ruinous, nets no costs at all, and shows the gains reversing on the unwind. Strip any one of those and the strategy is gone; strip all of them and you're holding ordinary expensive options again.

The published evidence on who actually cashes out is not close. Large-sample studies of retail options trading find aggregate losses in the billions; around earnings events — the most popular retail options trade — buyer returns run −5% to −14% per event on average; and the fine-grained brokerage-data studies of persistent day traders find 97% lose money over time, with no detectable learning curve. The winners are the sellers collecting the variance premium and the market makers collecting the spread. The house is not a metaphor here; it files quarterly reports.

Why people believe it anyway

Survivorship at industrial scale. A negative-expectation bet with a fat right tail is a screenshot factory: the 0.1% who hit publish, the 99.9% who bled out don't, and every viewer's brain does the rest. "Capped downside" is the most expensive phrase in retail finance — the cap is 100%, and you pay it a piece at a time, every week, forever.

What would resurrect it

On the buy side: we couldn't construct one, and not for lack of trying — this investigation was adversarial by design, and every framing (unconditional, regime-conditional, squeeze-event-driven) died on its own terms. The honest statement is that the persistent edge in options is on the sell side of the variance premium — and the seller owns the tail risk, which is precisely why the premium exists and why "just sell options" is a different way to blow up, not a free lunch. If someone publishes a squeeze-event definition with no look-ahead, a large-cap cost-netted universe, and positive out-of-sample returns, we will read it with genuine interest and our red pen.
