Autopsy №004: NBA back-to-back fatigue betting

SPECIMEN
Schedule-fatigue betting - fading tired teams on the second night of a back-to-back
CLAIM
Teams on zero rest are worse than the market prices them, so bet against the tired team on back-to-backs.
VERDICT
DEAD
CAUSE OF DEATH
The sharp closing line already prices schedule fatigue. Every real cut of the data comes back flat to negative, and the one nominally positive slice disappears after costs.
SAMPLE
19,637 NBA games · 15+ seasons, 2007-2023 · sportsbook closing moneylines, de-vigged to fair probabilities
DATE OF DEATH
August 2026

The pitch

Every broadcast has the graphic: team X is on the second night of a back-to-back, tired legs, short shootaround, maybe a flight in at 3am. The claim is that the market underweights this fatigue, particularly on the road and especially at altitude, where visiting legs are tired and lungs are also working harder than usual. Fade the tired team and collect the gap between what the market prices and what actually happens on the court. It's a mechanical, apparently exploitable edge: the schedule is public, the back-to-back dates are known weeks in advance, and the "tired legs" story is intuitive enough that it shows up in pregame commentary as settled fact rather than a hypothesis someone should test.

The test

19,637 NBA games across 15+ seasons, 2007-2023, priced against sportsbook closing moneylines de-vigged to fair (no-vig) probabilities, so the test is against the market's own best estimate rather than a naive line. Rest days, back-to-back status, rest-differential between the two teams, and altitude were all computed point-in-time, using only information that existed before tip-off. Every trade was charged a 1.88% cost haircut, half of the sample's 3.77% mean overround, a realistic estimate of what crossing the vig actually costs.

Two harness controls confirm the test isn't rigged to fail: fading a hash-randomized side scores about zero (t = -0.21), and fading every home team scores the mirror image of fading every away team (t = +/-0.38). Both are exactly what an unbiased test should produce on a coin flip and on a symmetric split, which means the harness has no built-in tilt toward finding or hiding an edge. If a real fatigue edge existed in this data, this harness would find it.

The result

Nothing survives. Every cut of back-to-back fatigue comes back flat to negative:

  • All back-to-backs (n=7,956): -0.32% edge per trade gross (t = -0.63), -2.20% net of costs.
  • Away back-to-backs only (n=5,208): -0.24% (t = -0.39).
  • Home back-to-backs only (n=2,748): -0.46% (t = -0.53).
  • Back-to-back against an opponent rested 2+ days (n=5,352): -0.46% (t = -0.77).
  • Rest disadvantage of 2+ days (n=2,410): -0.64% (t = -0.70).

None of these are statistically distinguishable from zero on their own, but they are consistently signed the wrong way for the strategy: fading the tired team loses money on average, it doesn't make it. Five different cuts of the same underlying idea, sliced by venue and by rest differential, and not one of them points the direction the pitch requires. That consistency matters more than any single t-stat: a real, priced-out effect should look like this, flat noise around zero regardless of how you slice it, rather than one slice working and the others not.

The one exception looked, briefly, like something: fading visiting teams at altitude (Denver and Utah, n=1,303) came back gross +1.96% per trade (t = +1.62, still short of significance). But net of the 1.88% cost haircut that shrinks to +0.08%, essentially zero, and more than 40% of that entire P&L is carried by a single season. That's not an edge, it's a concentration trap: one hot year wearing an edge's clothing. A result that depends on one season out of fifteen is a result that hasn't been tested yet.

Why people believe it anyway

NBA schedules are public information, and "tired team" is a story everyone can tell after the fact: you remember the back-to-back road loss that fit the narrative, not the dozens of back-to-backs that were unremarkable. Selective memory does the rest of the work; nobody keeps a running tally of the back-to-backs where the "tired" team won comfortably, because a boring result isn't a story.

Meanwhile the market for NBA games is one of the most heavily bet and heavily modeled in sports, and rest, travel, and altitude are exactly the kind of mechanical, schedule-known variables that get priced first and most completely, long before a bettor sits down to think about them. Fatigue is real for the players. It just isn't news to the closing line, which is set by people whose job is to already know the schedule.

What would resurrect it

Not a re-run of the general fatigue thesis; the general version died cleanly across every rest and venue split we have. The narrower altitude claim is the only piece with any life left in it, and even that piece currently rests on gross numbers that don't clear costs and a season count too thin to trust. A pre-registered replication of the altitude effect specifically, tested out-of-sample across multiple additional seasons rather than carried by one, and shown to survive realistic costs rather than netting to about zero, would be worth a second autopsy. We would run it if a live specimen showed up meeting those terms.
