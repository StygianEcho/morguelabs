Autopsy №006: Post-earnings drift in large caps

SPECIMEN
Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) in liquid large-cap stocks - buying the beat and shorting the miss
CLAIM
Prices keep drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for days after the print, so buying the biggest beats and shorting the biggest misses captures drift the market hasn't finished pricing in.
VERDICT
DEAD
CAUSE OF DEATH
Out-of-sample, the drift in liquid large caps runs negative, not positive. The one configuration that looks positive drops the market-cap filter and lets five illiquid micro-caps, one of them a warrant, carry the return on trading costs no real order book would offer.
SAMPLE
Large caps (>$10B) with |EPS surprise| > 5% · walk-forward event study · honest post-announcement entry · 5-day holds
DATE OF DEATH
August 2026

The pitch

Post-earnings announcement drift is one of the oldest anomalies in the finance literature: after a company reports earnings, its stock price doesn't fully adjust on the announcement day. It keeps drifting the same direction for days or weeks afterward, as if the market needs time to finish digesting the surprise. Beat estimates and the stock keeps climbing after the pop; miss badly and it keeps sinking after the drop. The trade writes itself: rank stocks by the size of their earnings surprise, buy the biggest beats, short the biggest misses, hold for a short window, and collect the drift the market apparently left on the table. Unlike most retail strategies, this one comes with real academic pedigree, which is exactly why it keeps getting repackaged into signal services and screener subscriptions decades after it was first described. The pitch has no moving parts to distrust: earnings surprises are public, the direction of a beat or a miss is unambiguous, and a five-day hold sounds short enough to dodge whatever risk sits further out on the calendar. That simplicity is the whole sales pitch, and it's also exactly what a realistic backtest is built to test.

The test

We ran a walk-forward event study restricted to large caps, market capitalization above $10 billion, with an absolute earnings-per-share surprise greater than 5%, wide enough to catch a genuine surprise without cherry-picking extreme prints. Entry was set at the close of the day AFTER the announcement, not the announcement day itself: the announcement-day jump happens before most order flow can react to it, and counting that jump as capturable return is look-ahead bias dressed up as a backtest. Each position was held five trading days and closed net of realistic transaction costs.

The result

Every parameter combination came back negative in-sample, before out-of-sample testing even began. Pooled across the walk-forward folds, the out-of-sample result held: -0.69% per event (t = -2.57), 0 of 2 folds positive, win rate 45%. The short side, betting against the misses, was the worse half of the trade: -1.20% per event (t = -2.22). Two folds, two negative results, no sign of an edge waiting one fold over.

There is exactly one way to make PEAD look alive, and it's instructive precisely because of how it fails. Drop the $10B market-cap filter and let small, illiquid names into the sample, and the pooled out-of-sample number flips to a headline-looking +3.16%. But the statistic behind it, t = 1.40, doesn't clear significance, and more to the point, 52% of that entire return comes from five illiquid micro-caps, one of which isn't a stock at all but a warrant. The backtest assumed a 0.05%-per-side trading cost for these names; real slippage on illiquid micro-caps runs 1-5% per side. The apparent edge is a costing error, not a market inefficiency.

Why people believe it anyway

PEAD has real citations behind it going back decades, and that pedigree makes it far more persuasive than retail folklore: it's taught in finance courses, cited in textbooks, and shows up in "quant strategies that actually work" lists precisely because it once did, in a market with higher trading costs and far less competition for exactly this signal. The result also survives a casual backtest because casual backtests rarely restrict to liquid large caps and rarely charge realistic costs. Loosen either constraint and PEAD reappears, not because the anomaly is real again, but because illiquid names and unrealistic fills are exactly where a backtest goes to manufacture a false edge. A five-stock sample carrying more than half the pooled return, with a warrant mixed in among the "stocks," is the kind of detail a headline pooled number conveniently omits, and it's precisely the detail a screener selling the signal has no incentive to disclose.

What would resurrect it

Not a looser universe. The large-cap version of PEAD died cleanly: negative in-sample, negative out-of-sample on both folds, worse on the short side. What would count as new evidence is a point-in-time-universe test, no survivorship bias, no look-ahead in the constituent list, showing net-positive drift in genuinely liquid names once realistic transaction costs are charged, not a pooled return propped up by a handful of micro-caps trading at costs no real order could get.
