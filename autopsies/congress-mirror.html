Autopsy №001: Copying Congress's stock trades

Correction & expansion (2026-08-08). During pre-distribution review, our operator asked why the most-tracked member of Congress showed only twelve purchases in our archive. Good question: it exposed that our vendor's House feed had been ingested with only ~7 months of history — the original sample (4,215 filings) was about a sixth of the real record, and Senate-skewed. We rebuilt the archive from full per-member histories the same day (22,646 purchase filings, 299 filers, 2020–2026) and re-ran every number in this piece. The verdict stands. Several supporting statistics changed; they are corrected below with the original claims retracted explicitly rather than silently edited, and the star-member test gained enough power to upgrade from "underpowered" to a formal fail. The prior version of this page is preserved in the site's public git history.

Completeness pass (2026-08-10). We then censused our archive against the House Clerk's own filing indexes, wrote a parser and recovered the 757 missing electronic filings ourselves (validated against filings we already held), OCR-censused the 250 paper-filed scans (49 are "nothing to report"; 77% of the remaining rows belong to two members whose alphas are already measured at zero on thousands of trades), and re-ran everything. The sample grew to 25,159 purchases; every verdict held; the big-trade analysis gained a finding (below).

The pitch

You've seen the screenshots: a senator's suspiciously well-timed semiconductor purchase, a speaker's household beating every hedge fund on Earth. There are apps that will mirror congressional trades into your brokerage account automatically, newsletters that alert you the moment a disclosure drops, and a durable internet consensus that STOCK Act filings are a legal insider-trading feed — smart money, in public, for free.

The test

We took every congressional purchase disclosure from 2020 through mid-2026 — 25,159 filings by 208 members, 23,328 of them in common stock — and ran a standard event study on dividend-adjusted daily prices (~97% of tickers had usable data). The archive behind this number is censused against the House Clerk's own filing indexes: a commercial feed, plus 757 electronic filings we parsed directly from the Clerk's PDFs with a validated parser, plus an OCR census of the paper-filed residue with a sensitivity bound showing the unrecovered scans cannot alter any conclusion below. Senate filings come from the aggregator's separate feed, reach back to 2020 with deep history, and have not yet been independently indexed against the Senate eFD system — the one census we haven't run. Performance is measured as alpha versus the S&P 500, not raw return: a strategy that merely rides the index deserves no credit in a bull tape.

Two clocks matter, so we tested both:

  • Transaction-date anchor — buy the moment the member did. This is impossible in practice (you can't see the trade until it's filed), but it's the cleanest test of the core premise: is Congress smart money at all?
  • Disclosure-date anchor — buy when the filing becomes public. This is the trade the apps actually execute. In our sample the disclosure lag ran a median of 27 days, mean 45.

The result

Congress is not smart money. At the transaction-date anchor, the impossible best case, mirrored purchases underperform the S&P 500:

About 48% of these trades beat the index. Significantly negative at two of three horizons, positive at none — at an execution speed no subscriber will ever have. Whatever members know, it isn't showing up in their fills.

The actionable version — buying at disclosure — is where the corrected data got genuinely interesting. The gross average looks positive: +0.17% per trade at 21 trading days (t ≈ 2.5), fading toward nothing by day 42. Before you subscribe to anything, meet the anatomy of that average:

  • It's a lottery, not an edge. Remove the top 1% of trades and the remaining 99% run −0.30% (t = −5.2). Remove the top 2%: −0.56%. The median trade loses (−0.19%) and fewer than half beat the index.
  • It lives in one year. Exclude 2020 alone and the whole effect is +0.04% at t = 0.55 — statistically indistinguishable from zero.
  • It lives in a few names. Five members account for the bulk of the summed gains.
  • It nets to less than nothing. After a modest 0.20% round-trip cost, the average copy-trade earns −0.03%.

And note the shape of the whole picture: negative when you trade alongside the member, positive-gross only after the filing becomes public. Whatever that post-disclosure drift is — attention, copy-trading flow — it is a property of the disclosure event, not of congressional insight. The trackers may be measuring their own wake.

Retracted (2026-08-08): the original version of this piece claimed the year-level edge concentrated in 2022 and that >$250k purchases performed worst (−2.02%). Both were artifacts of the truncated archive: on full data the only clearly positive zero-lag year is 2020 (2023 ran −1.1% at t = −8.5), and the big-purchase story turned out to be more interesting than either claim — see below.

The big-trade tell (added 2026-08-10, on the completed archive)

On the complete record, purchases over $250k do show something at the moment of purchase: +1.54% over 21 trading days (t = +3.2, n = 298). Before anyone gets excited: three members account for ~85% of the summed gains, the average member's big-trade alpha is zero (clustered t = +0.08), and the effect exists only in 2020–2022 — since 2023 it's been negative. But here is the part worth the price of admission: measure those same big trades from the disclosure date — the first moment you could copy them — and the alpha is +0.001% (t = 0.00). Not "small." Zero, to three decimal places. Whatever a handful of big trades once knew, the ~27-day disclosure lag launders it completely before the filing reaches your inbox. The tracker apps are selling you the receipt after the meal has been eaten.

Why people believe it anyway

Because the story is perfect and the counting is selective. A committee chair buying a defense stock before an appropriations bill is a screenshot; the twenty thousand mundane purchases that hug or lag the index are not. Nobody posts the full distribution, so the audience only ever sees the right tail. This is survivorship bias with a congressional seal on it.

But what about Pelosi? (added 2026-08-08 · re-run same day on the corrected archive)

It's the strongest objection to everything above: an average can be dumb while one member is smart, and the tracker products don't sell you the average — they sell you a name. So before distributing this piece, we tested the name-picking claim directly — pre-registered, thresholds locked before the data ran — and then re-ran it on the corrected full archive.

First, the anecdote itself. The most famous portfolio in Congress consists of 78 purchase filings in six and a half years — nearly half options, for which disclosures carry no strike, expiry, or premium (you cannot replicate what you cannot see), and 41 common-stock buys concentrated in mega-cap tech. Her rankable in-sample record: +0.79% per trade, t = 0.53 — statistically indistinguishable from zero. Her out-of-sample trades since 2024: −1.0%. The legend is not auditable as skill, and the part that is auditable is noise.

Second, the version the trackers actually sell — rank members by past mirror-alpha, follow the winners forward. On the full archive this test is properly powered: 71 of 208 filers have twenty-plus rankable stock purchases. Rank them on 2020–2023, follow the top fifteen out-of-sample:

  • Nine of the fifteen stars filed zero stock purchases out-of-sample (two more filed exactly one) — including the #1 (+21% per trade in-sample), who left Congress. Star-following fails operationally before it fails statistically.
  • The stars who kept trading: −0.40% per trade at 21 trading days, −1.52% at 42.
  • The bottom fifteen — the members the ranking says to avoid — ran +0.10%. The top-minus-bottom spread is inverted.
  • All four pre-registered checks fail. With the corrected archive this is no longer "underpowered" — it is a powered, formal fail.

Past member rank does not predict future member rank; where it points at all, it points backwards. The tracker sells you a story, and the full data audits it — badly.

What would resurrect it

We ran the member-selection revival ourselves — twice, the second time on the corrected full archive with real statistical power. It fails. What would still change our verdict: a pre-registered member-selection rule validated on out-of-sample filings from here forward, or a committee-timed event study with genuine identification. And one honest loose end: the gross post-disclosure drift is real enough to deserve study — as the market microstructure of attention, not as congressional skill. If someone shows it surviving costs outside its 2020/2022 tails, we'll publish that with the same prominence as the burial.
