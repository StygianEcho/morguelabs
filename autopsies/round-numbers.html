Autopsy №002: Round-number support & resistance

SPECIMEN
Psychological price levels — round-number support and resistance
CLAIM
Humans anchor on round numbers, so prices stall, bounce, and reverse at levels like $2,000 and $2,500. Trade the levels.
VERDICT
DEAD
CAUSE OF DEATH
Reversal behavior at round numbers is statistically indistinguishable from anywhere else on the chart. A placebo grid of deliberately meaningless levels performs identically.
SAMPLE
77,862 hourly ETH-USD bars · 2017–2026 · price range $82 → $4,935
DATE OF DEATH
July 2026

The pitch

It's in every technical-analysis course ever sold: markets are made of humans, humans think in round numbers, so round numbers become self-fulfilling battlegrounds. Support at $2,000. Resistance at $2,500. Draw the lines, wait for the bounce. It's intuitive, it's teachable, and every chart seems to confirm it — because a chart that has visited $2,000 many times will, of course, show many touches of $2,000.

The test

Nine years of hourly ETH-USD data — 77,862 bars covering a price journey from $82 to $4,935, which sweeps price across hundreds of round levels of every size. The design was built to be hard to fool:

  • Mechanical grids only. Levels at every multiple of $25, $50, $100, $250, $500, and $1,000 — no human drawing lines after seeing the chart, which is where most support/resistance "evidence" is manufactured.
  • A visit-time null. Price spends more time in some regions than others, so raw touch-counting flatters whatever levels price happened to loiter near. Every statistic is measured against how often price was near a level in the first place.
  • A placebo control. This is the part that matters: we re-ran the entire study on a grid shifted by a third of the spacing — deliberately meaningless levels like $2,333 — with the same tests. If "round" levels and arbitrary levels score the same, roundness was never doing anything.

The result

Nothing. Three findings, each fatal on its own:

  • Reversal rates are flat. The most direct test — does price reverse more often near a round level than far from one? — comes back with lift factors of 0.97 to 1.02 across every grid size. That's a rounding error around "no effect," not an edge.
  • The placebo passes everything the rounds pass. Where isolated statistics looked significant, the shifted grid of meaningless levels produced z-scores of the same magnitude. That's the signature of multiple-testing noise: run enough tests and something always glows. If your edge also appears at $2,333, it was never about $2,500.
  • The one real pattern isn't roundness. Forward returns around levels showed a mild mean-reversion tint — but it appears uniformly across all grid sizes rather than concentrating at the big psychological numbers, which means it's ordinary short-horizon mean reversion that exists everywhere on the chart. And its gross magnitude fails to clear a realistic ~30 basis-point round-trip trading cost anyway.

Why people believe it anyway

Because confirmation is free and disconfirmation requires a null model. Every time price bounces near a round number, the level "held." Every time it doesn't, the level "broke" — which is also treated as information. A theory that wins both ways isn't a theory; it's a Rorschach test with gridlines. The placebo grid is the antidote, and it's the one test no TA course will ever run for you.

What would resurrect it

A pre-registered replication showing round-level effects that (a) beat the visit-time null, (b) beat the placebo grid, and (c) survive costs — on any liquid asset. Order-book-level mechanisms (e.g., stop-loss clustering visible in depth data) are a different, more specific hypothesis than "draw lines at round numbers," and would need their own test. We'd run it if the depth data made it cheap.
