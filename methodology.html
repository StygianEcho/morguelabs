What counts as a kill

Anyone can declare a strategy dead. The word only means something if the test would have been allowed to say "alive."

Every autopsy published here comes from a test built under the following rules. They exist because each one closes a specific door through which dead strategies sneak back into the world looking alive — and most published backtests, academic and retail alike, leave several of them open.

The rules

1. Criteria are fixed before the data comes in

What result would count as an edge — the metric, the threshold, the sample — is written down before the test runs. Deciding after you've seen the numbers is how every "worked in backtest" strategy got its résumé.

2. Out-of-sample or it didn't happen

Where a strategy has tunable parameters, validation is walk-forward: tune on one period, score on data the tuning never saw, repeat across time. A strategy only gets credit for the periods it hadn't met.

3. Costs are real, and they are the usual murder weapon

Every test charges realistic all-in costs — commissions, spread, slippage — for the instrument in question. An enormous fraction of published "edges" are alive gross and dead net; we report which side of that line a specimen died on, because it changes what would resurrect it.

4. Survivorship hygiene

Universes are constructed as they existed at the time, not filtered through who's still listed today. Backtests over today's index members are séances: you're only interviewing the survivors.

5. Null and placebo controls

Where the hypothesis allows it, the same test runs against a deliberately meaningless version — shifted price levels, random event dates, hash-picked sides. If the placebo scores like the signal, the signal is noise wearing a costume. We consider this the single highest-value habit in the building.

6. Concentration checks

If most of a strategy's profit lives in one year, one regime, or five tickers, that's reported as the finding — because it is the finding. An average dragged positive by a handful of outliers is a story about those outliers, not about the rule.

7. Statistics with the flattery removed

Clustered events are not independent observations; t-statistics get treated with corresponding suspicion. Medians ride alongside means. A significant result with a coin-flip hit rate and a median of zero gets called what it is.

What a kill means — and doesn't

A kill is specific: this construction of the idea, on this data, under this cost model, is dead. It is not a proof that no cousin of the idea could ever work — which is why every autopsy ends with a "what would resurrect it" section stating the falsifiable conditions under which we'd reverse the verdict. That section is the difference between science and a hit piece.

And one epistemic disclosure in the other direction: a desk that finds a living edge doesn't publish it — live edges decay on contact with daylight, and dead ones don't. That's the honest reason a morgue can afford to be honest: our incentives only work because everything here is already dead.
